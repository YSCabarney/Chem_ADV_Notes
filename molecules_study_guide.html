Valence Electrons

What are valence electrons?

The valence electrons are the electrons in an atom's outermost energy level (the highest occupied shell). They are the only electrons involved in chemical bonding — the core electrons deep inside the atom just sit there.

⚽ Real World → Soccer

Think of an atom like a team. The core electrons are the bench — they never leave. The valence electrons are your starters — they're the ones that make plays, form connections, and determine how reactive your team (atom) is in a match (reaction).

How to find valence electrons

  • For main group elements, the number of valence electrons equals the group number (column) on the periodic table.
  • Group 1 → 1 valence electron, Group 2 → 2, Group 13 → 3, Group 14 → 4 ... Group 18 → 8
  • Valence electrons live in the s and p subshells of the outermost energy level.
  • d and f subshell electrons are generally not counted as valence electrons for main group elements.
⚗️ Real World Connection

Your body runs on chemistry driven entirely by valence electrons. When your muscles contract on the pitch, calcium ions (Ca²⁺) — formed because calcium loses its 2 valence electrons — trigger the protein fibers in your muscles to slide past each other. No valence electrons, no sprint.

🔢

The Octet Rule

The rule

Atoms of main group elements are most stable when they have 8 valence electrons in their outermost shell — a full octet, like the noble gases. Atoms will gain, lose, or share electrons in order to reach this "happy eight." Two exceptions: hydrogen and helium, which are full with just 2.

Inert elements: already happy

When an element already has 8 valence electrons (the noble gases: He, Ne, Ar, Kr ...), it is inert — it doesn't need to react. Noble gases are the chemistry equivalent of an athlete who already has everything they need — they're not trading or sharing anything with anyone.

Strategies atoms use to reach 8

  • Lose electrons (usually metals) → become a positively charged cation (+)
  • Gain electrons (usually nonmetals) → become a negatively charged anion (−)
  • Share electrons (usually nonmetal + nonmetal) → covalent bond

Electron Configuration

Review

Quick review — subshells and notation

  • Electrons fill subshells in order: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d ...
  • s holds max 2 electrons, p holds max 6, d holds max 10
  • Write the energy level, then the subshell letter, then a superscript with the number of electrons.
Chlorine (Cl, 17e⁻): 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁵

Noble gas notation (shorthand)

Because full electron configurations get long, chemists use the previous noble gas in brackets as a shortcut, then write only the remaining outer electrons.

Cl full: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁵ → Cl shorthand: [Ne] 3s²3p⁵

This is useful because the electrons after the bracket are the valence electrons — the ones that do the bonding.

⚽ Connection to Soccer

Noble gas notation is like a scouting report shorthand. Instead of listing every game a player has ever played, you just write "[National Team level]" and then describe what's new and relevant. The bracket captures everything that's already established.

Lewis Dot Notation — Single Atoms

What it is

Lewis dot notation is a quick visual way to show how many valence electrons an atom has. You write the element symbol, then place dots around it — one for each valence electron. The arrangement of dots tells you exactly how that atom will bond.

📋 HOW TO DRAW A LEWIS DOT DIAGRAM — Atoms
1

Find the valence electrons

Use the periodic table group number for main group elements. That number = the number of dots you'll place.

Carbon (Group 14) → 4 valence electrons → 4 dots
2

Write the element symbol

Put the chemical symbol in the center. This represents the nucleus plus all core electrons.

Write: C
3

Place dots clockwise, one at a time first

Start at the top. Go clockwise: top → right → bottom → left. Place one dot per side before you pair any up. Only pair dots (put 2 on a side) once every side has at least one.

Li (1 dot): one dot on top · Be (2 dots): top + right · B (3 dots): top, right, bottom ...
4

Continue placing / pairing until all dots are placed

Dots 5–8 pair up with the existing single dots. A paired dot on a side = a lone pair. An unpaired dot = an open bonding site.

Oxygen (6 dots): top-pair, right-pair, one bottom, one left → 2 unpaired = forms 2 bonds

Ionic Bonds

The core idea

An ionic bond forms when one atom transfers one or more electrons to another atom. The atom that loses electrons becomes a positively charged cation (+). The atom that gains electrons becomes a negatively charged anion (−). Opposite charges attract — that's the bond.

  • Metals tend to lose electrons → form cations
  • Nonmetals tend to gain electrons → form anions
  • General rule: metal + nonmetal → ionic bond
⚽ Real World → Sports Science

When you sweat on the pitch, you lose electrolytes — sodium (Na⁺), potassium (K⁺), chloride (Cl⁻), magnesium (Mg²⁺). These are all ions held together in your sports drink by ionic bonds. That's why the drink tastes salty. Those ions are critical for nerve signals and muscle contractions. No ionic chemistry = no sprint, no shot, no tackle.

⚗️ Ionic Bonds in Daily Life

Table salt (NaCl) is the classic ionic compound. Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) in antacids and chalk. Iron oxide (Fe₂O₃) — rust. Sodium fluoride (NaF) — toothpaste. Ionic compounds are everywhere because metals and nonmetals are extremely common partners.

Electronegativity & Ionization Energy

  • Electronegativity: how strongly an atom pulls electrons toward itself. High difference between atoms (≥ 1.7) → one atom wins the electron completely → ionic bond.
  • Ionization energy: how much energy it takes to remove an electron. Metals have low ionization energy (easy to remove) — that's why they give up electrons readily.

Writing Ionic Formulas (Formula Units)

What is a formula unit?

A formula unit is the simplest whole-number ratio of ions in an ionic compound. The goal: the net charge must equal zero.

📋 HOW TO WRITE AN IONIC FORMULA — 5 Steps
1

Identify the charge of each ion

Use the periodic table group to determine how many electrons will be lost or gained. Metals lose electrons (positive charge), nonmetals gain electrons (negative charge).

Ca is in Group 2 → loses 2e⁻ → Ca²⁺  |  Br is in Group 17 → gains 1e⁻ → Br⁻
2

Write cation first, then anion

Cation (metal, +) always comes first. Anion (nonmetal, −) goes second.

Ca²⁺ and Br⁻ → write as Ca²⁺ Br⁻
3

"Cross the charges" — determine the ratio

The number part of each ion's charge becomes the subscript of the other ion. This ensures the charges balance to zero.

Ca²⁺ and Br⁻ → the 2 goes to Br, the 1 (silent) goes to Ca → CaBr₂
4

Reduce to lowest whole-number ratio

If both subscripts share a common factor, reduce them. This keeps the formula as simple as possible.

Ba₂S₂ → both divisible by 2 → simplifies to BaS
5

Check: net charge = zero

Multiply each ion's charge by its subscript. Add them together — they must cancel to zero.

Al₂O₃: Al = 3+ × 2 = 6+, O = 2− × 3 = 6− → 6+ + 6− = 0 ✓

Polyatomic Ions

What are they?

A polyatomic ion is a group of atoms covalently bonded together that has an overall positive or negative charge — meaning it acts as a single ion. They can bond with single ions or other polyatomic ions to form ionic compounds.

Common polyatomic ions you need to know

HCO₃⁻BicarbonateBaking soda, blood pH buffer

⚽ Real World → Sports Nutrition

Your sports drink contains sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃) — literally baking soda — because HCO₃⁻ is a pH buffer. During intense exercise, lactic acid builds up in your muscles and causes that burning feeling. The bicarbonate ion neutralizes excess acid and extends your performance. Elite athletes have been known to take sodium bicarbonate before competition. That's a polyatomic ion working for you.

Parentheses in formulas — why?

When a formula needs more than one polyatomic ion, put the ion in parentheses and add the subscript outside. The subscript outside multiplies everything inside the parentheses.

Al₂(CO₃)₃ → Aluminum: 2 atoms, Carbon: 1×3 = 3, Oxygen: 3×3 = 9 total atoms

Covalent Bonds

The core idea

A covalent bond forms when two atoms share electrons — neither one takes them completely. Both atoms' valence shells overlap, and the shared electrons "belong" to both. This typically happens between two nonmetals.

⚗️ Covalent Bonds Are Everywhere

Water (H₂O): the molecule your body is 60% made of. Carbon dioxide (CO₂): the greenhouse gas reshaping the planet's climate. Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆): the sugar your muscles burn for energy during a match. DNA: the covalently bonded molecule that carries your genetic code. Covalent bonds are the backbone of all living things and most of organic chemistry.

Single, Double, and Triple Bonds

Atoms can share more than one pair of electrons. The more electrons shared, the stronger and shorter the bond.

More bonds = stronger AND shorter. A triple bond is harder to break than a single bond. That's why N₂ (the air you breathe — 78% of it) is so stable and unreactive.

⚽ Real World → ATP and Muscle Energy

Your muscles use ATP (adenosine triphosphate) for instant energy. The "tri" means three phosphate groups connected by high-energy covalent bonds. When those bonds break during intense bursts — a sprint, a shot — energy is released immediately. The bonds in some biological molecules literally store energy like a compressed spring.

Lewis Dot Structures — Covalent Molecules

The concept

Just as we drew Lewis dots for single atoms, we can draw them for molecules. In a molecular Lewis structure, a line between two atoms = one shared pair (one covalent bond). Dots still around the symbols = lone pairs (nonbonded electrons).

📋 HOW TO DRAW A LEWIS STRUCTURE — Covalent Molecules
1

Count total valence electrons from all atoms

Add up the valence electrons from every atom in the molecule. This is your electron budget — you must use all of them.

H₂O: O has 6, each H has 1 → 6 + 1 + 1 = 8 total valence electrons
2

Identify and place the central atom

The central atom is the least electronegative one (farthest from the top-right corner of the periodic table). Exception: hydrogen is never the central atom — it only bonds once.

In H₂O → O is the central atom (H can't be central)
3

Connect atoms with single bonds first

Draw single lines (one bond = 2e⁻) from the central atom to each outer atom. Each line uses 2 electrons from your budget.

O−H and O−H uses 4 electrons. 8 − 4 = 4 electrons remaining.
4

Distribute remaining electrons as lone pairs

Fill outer atoms' octets first (H just needs 2), then place remaining electrons on the central atom.

O has 4 remaining electrons → place as 2 lone pairs on oxygen
5

Check octets — upgrade to double/triple bonds if needed

If the central atom doesn't have a full octet, convert lone pairs from adjacent atoms into additional bonds. Every electron must be accounted for.

H₂O check: O has 2 bonds + 2 lone pairs = 8 electrons ✓   Each H has 2 electrons ✓
⚗️ Real World — Why Water is Weird (and Why We're Alive)

The Lewis structure of water (H₂O) shows O with 2 lone pairs and 2 bonds to H. Those lone pairs push the molecule into a bent shape, which creates polarity — one side slightly negative, one slightly positive. That polarity is why water has surface tension, why it dissolves salt and sugar, why it's a liquid at room temperature. The bent molecular shape, dictated by the Lewis structure, is basically the reason life on Earth exists.

Polarity

What makes a molecule polar?

When two atoms in a covalent bond have different electronegativities, they don't share electrons equally — the electrons spend more time near the more electronegative atom. This creates a partial negative charge (δ−) on one end and a partial positive charge (δ+) on the other.

Nonpolar Covalent Bond

Electrons shared equally. Same element bonded (like C−C, O₂, N₂), or very similar electronegativity (difference < 0.5). No partial charges. Molecule is electrically symmetric.

C−C  ·  H−H  ·  O═O

Polar Covalent Bond

Electrons shared unequally. Difference in electronegativity 0.5–1.7. The more electronegative atom gets δ−. Creates a dipole — a molecular magnet of sorts.

H−O  ·  H−N  ·  H−F
⚗️ Real World — Polarity and Dissolving

"Like dissolves like." Polar solvents (like water) dissolve polar and ionic solutes (salt, sugar, sports drink electrolytes). Nonpolar solvents (like oil) dissolve nonpolar substances. That's why oil and water don't mix. When you're mixing a protein shake, the protein powder dissolves in water because proteins are polar molecules.

⚖️

Ionic vs. Covalent — The Comparison

⚡ Ionic Bonds

  • Electrons are transferred
  • Metal + Nonmetal
  • Forms ions (+ and −)
  • High melting/boiling points
  • Brittle solid at room temp
  • Dissolves in water (conducts electricity when dissolved)
  • Examples: NaCl, CaCO₃, MgO

🤝 Covalent Bonds

  • Electrons are shared
  • Nonmetal + Nonmetal
  • Forms molecules (neutral)
  • Lower melting/boiling points
  • Can be gas, liquid, or soft solid
  • Dissolving varies (may or may not)
  • Examples: H₂O, CO₂, C₆H₁₂O₆

🔗 How Everything Connects

1️⃣
Electron configuration tells you how electrons are arranged → reveals the valence electrons in the outermost shell.
2️⃣
Valence electrons + the octet rule explain why atoms bond at all — they're trying to reach 8.
3️⃣
Lewis dot diagrams visualize valence electrons, which predicts bonding behavior and the type of bond.
4️⃣
Electronegativity difference determines bond type: large difference → ionic (transfer), moderate → polar covalent, small → nonpolar covalent.

Polarity of a molecule affects its physical properties — solubility, boiling point, biological behavior. The shape (from Lewis structures) determines polarity.

// TAP A QUESTION TO SEE THE ANSWER
+
How many valence electrons does nitrogen (N, Group 15) have, and what does its Lewis dot diagram look like?
Nitrogen has 5 valence electrons. The Lewis dot has: 1 lone pair (top), and 3 single dots (right, bottom, left). It needs 3 more electrons to reach 8 → it forms 3 bonds.
+
When calcium (Ca²⁺) bonds with chlorine (Cl⁻), what is the ionic formula? Show your work.
Ca is 2+, Cl is 1−. Cross the charges: the 2 becomes Cl's subscript, the 1 (silent) becomes Ca's subscript → CaCl₂. Check: 2+ + (2 × 1−) = 0 ✓
+
What type of bond does CO₂ have, and why? Is it polar or nonpolar?
Covalent bonds (nonmetal + nonmetal). Carbon forms 2 double bonds with oxygen (C═O). Each C═O bond IS polar (O is more electronegative), but the molecule is linear, so the dipoles cancel → CO₂ is a nonpolar molecule overall, even though it has polar bonds.
+
What is the difference between a polar covalent bond and an ionic bond in terms of electron behavior?
In a polar covalent bond, electrons are shared unequally — the more electronegative atom pulls them closer (partial charges, δ+ and δ−). In an ionic bond, the difference is so large that one atom takes the electron(s) completely, forming full ions (Na⁺, Cl⁻). Ionic bond = complete transfer. Polar covalent = unequal sharing.
+
What is a polyatomic ion? Give one real-world example and explain its role.
A group of covalently bonded atoms that together carry an overall charge. Example: HCO₃⁻ (bicarbonate) — present in your blood as a pH buffer. During intense exercise, your muscles produce lactic acid. Bicarbonate neutralizes the excess H⁺ ions, preventing dangerous acidification of your blood.
