The Periodic Table

One scientist, one dream, and 118 elements that turned out to follow a hidden pattern. Here's what that pattern reveals — and why your electron configuration notes from last week are the key to understanding all of it.

← Connected to last week

Last week you mapped out how electrons arrange themselves in shells and subshells — the specific pattern called electron configuration. Here's the payoff: the periodic table is literally organized by electron configuration. Each row (period) represents a new electron shell. Each column (group) shares the same number of valence electrons — the outermost electrons that drive almost all of chemistry. Everything you learned last week is the reason this table works.

The big picture

Why organize elements at all?

The human mind loves organization. We sort songs into playlists, food into pantries, and clothes into closets — because finding things is faster when there's a system. Scientists face the same problem with matter: there are 118 known elements, each with its own mass, reactivity, and behavior. Without a map, you'd have to memorize every element's behavior from scratch.

In the 1860s, a Russian chemist named Dmitri Mendeleev noticed that when elements were arranged by increasing atomic mass, their properties repeated in a predictable pattern. According to legend, the full arrangement came to him in a dream. He published it in 1869 — leaving deliberate gaps for elements he predicted must exist but hadn't been discovered yet. He was right about all of them.

Key insight

The modern periodic table organizes elements by atomic number (number of protons), not atomic mass as Mendeleev originally did. This small correction fixed the few places where Mendeleev's ordering didn't match observed properties.

Protons = atomic number = identity

The number of protons never changes for a given element. Gain or lose a proton and you have a completely different element. That's why atomic number — not mass, not electrons — defines what element you're looking at.

Layout of the table

Periods, groups, and why they matter

The periodic table isn't just a list — its shape encodes information. The position of an element tells you about its electron configuration before you even look it up.

Periods (rows)

A period is a horizontal row. There are 7 periods. As you move across a period left to right, you're adding one proton — and one electron — at a time. That electron goes into the same energy shell. When a shell fills up, the next period begins.

← Last week's connection: Period 1 = shell 1 fills. Period 2 = shell 2 fills. Period 3 = shell 3 fills. The period number matches the outermost shell number in the electron configuration.

Groups (columns)

A group (also called a family) is a vertical column, numbered 1–18. Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons — the electrons in the outermost shell. That's why elements in the same group behave so similarly in chemical reactions.

Real-world example — Group 1

Sodium (Na) and potassium (K) are both in Group 1. Both have exactly one valence electron. Both react explosively with water. That's not a coincidence — it's the electron configuration they share. Doctors measure sodium and potassium levels in blood for the same reason: they behave chemically the same way in your body.

Element categories

Metals, nonmetals, and metalloids

One of Mendeleev's first observations was that elements could be sorted into metals and nonmetals based on physical properties. A third category — metalloids — sits in between, on a staircase-shaped border on the right side of the table.

Why are metalloids useful in electronics?

Metalloids like silicon and germanium are semiconductors — they conduct electricity under certain conditions but not others. That switchable behavior is exactly what makes transistors (and therefore all modern computers and smartphones) possible. A semiconductor can act as an on/off switch for electrical current, which is how binary code (0s and 1s) gets physically encoded in a chip. Nearly every piece of digital technology you own depends on this property.

Inside the atom

Atomic number and mass number

The element box gives you two numbers that together let you count every subatomic particle in a neutral atom.

The three key equations

Atomic number (Z) = number of protons
In a neutral atom: electrons = protons = atomic number
Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons → so neutrons = A − Z

Worked example — Chlorine-37

Chlorine's atomic number is 17. An atom of chlorine-37 has a mass number of 37.

  • Protons = atomic number = 17
  • Electrons = protons (neutral atom) = 17
  • Neutrons = mass number − atomic number = 37 − 17 = 20

Subatomic particle calculator

Mass number vs. atomic mass

The mass number is always a whole number — it's a count of protons + neutrons in one specific atom. The atomic mass on the periodic table is a decimal because it's the weighted average across all naturally occurring isotopes of that element. They're related but not the same.

Isotopes

Same element, different mass

Every atom of carbon has exactly 6 protons — that's what makes it carbon. But a carbon atom can have 6, 7, or 8 neutrons. These variants are called isotopes: atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons (and therefore different mass numbers).

Two ways to write an isotope

Chemists have two notations. Either works — you need to recognize both:

  • Hyphen notation: element name + dash + mass number → carbon-14, uranium-235
  • Nuclear symbol notation: mass number as superscript upper-left, atomic number as subscript lower-left of the symbol
Real-world application: radiocarbon dating

Carbon-14 is a radioactive isotope that forms naturally in the upper atmosphere and gets absorbed by all living things. When an organism dies, it stops taking in new carbon, and the carbon-14 already inside begins to decay at a precise, known rate (its half-life is 5,730 years). By measuring how much carbon-14 remains in an artifact — a fossil bone, a piece of ancient cloth — archaeologists can calculate when that organism died. This is how the age of items like the Dead Sea Scrolls and ancient Egyptian mummies was confirmed.

Real-world application: radioactive isotopes in medicine

Unstable (radioactive) isotopes emit detectable energy as they decay. Nuclear medicine uses this to image the inside of the body. For example, technetium-99m is the most widely used medical radioisotope — it's injected into a patient, travels to specific organs, and emits gamma rays that a scanner detects to produce images of organ function. It's used in millions of diagnostic scans every year for cancer detection, heart disease, and bone conditions. X-ray scanners at airports use a similar principle: different elements and isotopes absorb radiation in predictable amounts, letting technicians identify hidden materials by how much energy they absorb.

Average atomic mass

Why atomic mass is always a decimal

The atomic mass on the periodic table is never a clean whole number (with rare exceptions) because it's the weighted average of all naturally occurring isotopes. More abundant isotopes pull the average closer to their mass; rarer ones have less influence.

avg atomic mass = Σ (percent abundance × isotope mass)

Worked example — Carbon

Carbon has three natural isotopes. The two most significant:

Natural abundance of carbon isotopes
C-12 (98.9%)
C-12 = 98.9%  |  C-13 = 1.1%  |  C-14 = trace

Step-by-step calculation

(12 u × 0.989) + (13 u × 0.011) + (14 u × ~0.000)
= 11.868 + 0.143 + ~0
= 12.011 u ← matches the periodic table

Practice — Chlorine

Chlorine has two main isotopes: chlorine-35 (75.77% abundant) and chlorine-37 (24.23% abundant). Using the formula:

Try it yourself first, then check

(35 × 0.7577) + (37 × 0.2423) = 26.52 + 8.96 = 35.48 u — check the periodic table for Cl and you'll find 35.45 u (slight rounding from textbook vs. precise values).

Why do we use a weighted average instead of a simple average?

A simple average of chlorine's two isotopes would be (35 + 37) ÷ 2 = 36. But chlorine-35 is three times more abundant than chlorine-37, so the average should be pulled much closer to 35. A weighted average accounts for how often each isotope actually appears in nature — which is what matters for chemistry. When you buy a bottle of chemicals, the atoms inside follow natural abundance ratios, not a 50/50 split.

In the news — 2026

How isotopes are catching smugglers and forgers

Last week's density problem was about catching fake gold bars. This week's unit explains why isotope analysis goes even further. Every material on Earth has a characteristic isotopic fingerprint — the exact ratio of its naturally occurring isotopes. Forensic scientists now use mass spectrometry to read those fingerprints. Counterfeit gold can be caught not just by density, but by the ratio of gold isotopes (Au-197 is the only stable isotope — if anything else shows up, it's not pure gold). The same technique identified the origin of the polonium used to poison Russian dissident Alexander Litvinenko in 2006.

Connection: electron config explains all of these

Every trend above is explained by one thing: how strongly the nucleus attracts its outermost electrons, and how shielded those electrons are by inner shells. The further out the valence electrons are (more shells = bigger atom), the easier they are to pull away. This is electron configuration in action — last week wasn't review, it was foundation.

Key terms

Glossary

Atomic mass unit (u)
A unit of mass defined as exactly 1/12 the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Used to express masses of atoms and subatomic particles.
Atomic mass
The weighted average mass of all naturally occurring isotopes of an element, measured in atomic mass units (u).
Atomic number (Z)
The number of protons in the nucleus of one atom of an element. Defines the element — every atom with 79 protons is gold, no exceptions.
Average atomic mass
The average mass of all isotopes of an element, weighted by each isotope's percent abundance in nature.
Chemical symbol
A one- or two-letter abbreviation for an element, often derived from its Latin name (Au = aurum = gold).
Family / Group
A vertical column on the periodic table, numbered 1–18. Elements in the same group share the same number of valence electrons and have similar chemical properties.
Isotope
Atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, giving them different mass numbers.
Mass number (A)
The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of a specific isotope. Always a whole number; not found directly on the periodic table.
Metalloid
An element with properties intermediate between metals and nonmetals. Silicon and germanium are metalloids — the backbone of semiconductor electronics.
Period
A horizontal row on the periodic table, numbered 1–7. All elements in a period have the same number of electron shells.
Valence electrons
The electrons in an atom's outermost shell. These electrons determine how an element reacts chemically and explain why elements in the same group behave similarly.
Weighted average
An average that accounts for the relative frequency (abundance) of each value. In chemistry: Σ(isotope mass × percent abundance) = average atomic mass.
