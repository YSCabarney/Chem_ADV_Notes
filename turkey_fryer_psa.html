01 The Real-World Problem

Every Thanksgiving, fire departments across the country warn people about frying turkeys. Someone heats a big pot of oil, lowers in a turkey that is still frozen or wet, and the oil erupts out of the pot. If that oil reaches the burner flame, you get a fireball.

It looks like magic, but it's chemistry, and it's chemistry you already know from our bonding unit. Your job is to explain it so clearly that anyone watching or listening will never make this mistake.

⚠️ NO REAL DEMOS. EVER. Do not put water, ice, or anything else into hot oil, and do not light anything on fire. Use acting, drawings, props, toys, sound effects, or animation. A rubber chicken works great.
Hot oil · 350°F

02 The Science You MUST Show

Your PSA can be funny, dramatic, or weird, but it has to teach the science. Cover all five of these in your own words. This is where most of your points come from.

🧪

1. Start at the periodic table.

Name the elements in the "cast": water is H₂O (hydrogen + oxygen), and cooking oil is mostly long chains of carbon + hydrogen. Use the periodic table to say where they sit: nonmetals on the right side (hydrogen is the odd one out at the top left). Give each one's valence electrons.

Hint: valence electrons from the group number
For main-group elements, the group number tells you the valence electrons. H (Group 1) = 1 · C (Group 14) = 4 · O (Group 16) = 6. To reach an octet, oxygen needs 2 more electrons, so it shares with 2 hydrogens. That's why water is H₂O. Carbon needs 4 more, so it makes 4 bonds.
🔗

2. Water and oil both have covalent bonds, but one is polar.

Explain that nonmetals share electrons, which makes covalent bonds. Then use electronegativity to explain why the O–H bonds in water are polar but the C–H bonds in oil are nonpolar, and why that means they don't mix ("like dissolves like").

Hint: run the numbers in the Bond Calculator below
O = 3.44 and H = 2.20, a difference of 1.24. That's between 0.5 and 1.7, so it's polar covalent. Oxygen hogs the shared electrons (δ−) and the hydrogens end up δ+.
C = 2.55 and H = 2.20, a difference of 0.35. That's under 0.5, so it's nonpolar covalent: the electrons are shared almost evenly.
Polar water molecules stick to each other, not to nonpolar oil, so the two stay separate.
🌡️

3. The oil is WAY hotter than water's boiling point.

Compare the two temperatures and explain what that means for any water or ice that gets into the pot.

Hint: the numbers
Frying oil is usually heated to about 350°F (175°C). Water boils at 212°F (100°C). Because water doesn't mix into the oil, it stays as droplets and chunks. Water is also denser than oil, so it sinks to the bottom, where it gets hit with heat from every side.
💥

4. Water turns to steam instantly and expands.

Explain what happens when liquid water becomes a gas all at once, and why that throws the oil out of the pot.

Hint: how much bigger is steam?
When liquid water turns into steam, it takes up roughly 1,600–1,700 times more space. All of that gas shoots up through the oil and carries hot oil over the side of the pot. If the oil lands on the flame below, it catches fire.
🧯

5. Safety tips, with an IONIC hero.

Give at least two safety tips and explain why each one works. One has to be never throw water on a grease fire. Another has to feature an ionic compound that fights grease fires. Explain why it's ionic (a metal gives electrons to a nonmetal or a polyatomic ion) and point out where its elements are on the periodic table.

Hint: pick your ionic hero
🥄 Baking soda, NaHCO₃: Na⁺ (a Group 1 metal, far left) + HCO₃⁻ (the bicarbonate polyatomic ion). Poured on a small grease fire, it smothers the flames, and heat breaks it down to release CO₂ gas, which pushes away the O₂ the fire needs.
🧂 Table salt, NaCl: Na (Group 1 metal) gives 1 electron to Cl (Group 17 nonmetal). The difference is 3.16 − 0.93 = 2.23, well over 1.7, so it's ionic. A thick layer can smother a small fire.
🧯 Class K "wet chemical" fire extinguishers, the kind used in restaurant kitchens, spray potassium compounds like potassium acetate or potassium carbonate. K⁺ comes from a Group 1 metal.
Other good tips: fully thaw and dry the turkey · fry outdoors, away from the house · turn off the heat and cover with a lid to cut off oxygen (O₂) · don't overfill the pot · never leave it alone.
