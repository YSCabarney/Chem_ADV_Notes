Why?

Chemists call the periodic table their best friend. Besides atomic numbers and masses, its layout lets you predict an element's size, how tightly it holds its electrons, and how hard it pulls on electrons in a bond. Last week you learned that each period is a new electron shell and each group shares the same number of valence electrons. This week you'll use that to explain three periodic trends. Like most trends, they aren't perfect, but they are very useful.

By the end you should be able to: describe the trends in atomic radius, ionization energy and electronegativity; explain each trend using Coulombic attraction, electron shells and protons; and use the trends to explain a real-world problem.

Read this first: Coulombic attraction
Opposite charges attract. The attraction gets stronger when the charges are bigger and weaker when they are farther apart. In an atom, the positive nucleus pulls on the negative electrons. Inner electrons also sit between the nucleus and the outer electrons and partly block, or shield, that pull. Hint: going down a group, what happens to the number of electron shells?

In the news2025–2026

Salt-powered cars? Sodium-ion batteries hit the road

Your phone, laptop and most electric cars run on lithium-ion batteries. Lithium is mined in only a few places, and its price swings a lot. In 2026, CATL (the world's largest battery maker) and the carmaker Changan announced the first mass-produced passenger car built with sodium-ion batteries, and BYD is building sodium-ion factories too. Sodium is cheap and everywhere. It's half of table salt.

The catch: sodium sits right below lithium in Group 1. In a battery, the metal travels as an ion and has to squeeze in and out of the layers of the electrode every time you charge. Bigger particles need roomier electrodes, so sodium batteries use a different material called hard carbon, and they store a little less energy for their weight. The ions follow the same size trend as the atoms.

Ionization energy

Ionization energy is the amount of energy needed to remove one electron from an atom. The first ionization energy removes the outermost, most loosely held electron. It is the second number on each Model 1 card, in kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol).

Model 3 about 12 min

Electronegativity

Read this!

Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom's nucleus attracts electrons from a different atom when the two are joined in a covalent bond. A higher value means a stronger pull on the shared electrons. Electronegativity is calculated, not measured directly in the lab, so it has no unit. It is the third number on each Model 1 card (Pauling scale, 0 to 4).

In the newsMay 2026

"Forever chemicals" in our water

PFAS are a family of thousands of human-made chemicals used in nonstick pans, waterproof jackets, food wrappers and firefighting foam. They're called forever chemicals because they barely break down. Scientists have found them in rainwater, in drinking water, and in the blood of nearly every American who has been tested.

In 2024 the EPA set the first national drinking-water limits for two PFAS, PFOA and PFOS, at 4 parts per trillion. In May 2026 the EPA proposed keeping those two limits but giving some water systems until 2031 to meet them, and removing the limits on four other PFAS. Cleaning PFAS out of water is expensive because the molecules are so hard to destroy.

Why so tough? Every PFAS molecule is built on a chain of carbon atoms with fluorine atoms bonded all around it.

In the news2025–2026

The magnet shortage that stalled car factories

Neodymium magnets are the strongest permanent magnets made. They're in your earbuds, phone speakers and game-controller rumble motors, and in the motor of nearly every electric car. A small amount of dysprosium is added so the magnets keep working when a motor gets hot.

China makes about 90% of the world's rare earth magnets. In 2025 it restricted exports of several rare earth elements, including dysprosium and terbium. Carmakers around the world, including Ford and Suzuki, reported shortages, and some of those limits were still in place in 2026. Other countries are now racing to build their own supply.

Why is that hard? Rare earth elements are found mixed together in the same rocks, and they behave so much alike that separating them takes hundreds of repeated chemical steps.
