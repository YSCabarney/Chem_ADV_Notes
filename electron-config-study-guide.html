Before You Start

Quick Refresher: Inside the Atom

Every atom is built from three particles. Before we zoom in on where electrons live, make sure these three are solid.

The rule everything else depends on: the number of electrons in a neutral atom always equals the number of protons. It's that outer layer of electrons — not the nucleus — that decides how an atom behaves, bonds, and reacts. That's exactly what the rest of this guide maps out.

Concept One

Schrödinger's Cat: Why We Needed a New Model

Bohr's model drew electrons circling the nucleus in neat orbits, like planets around the sun. It's a useful sketch, but it's not what actually happens — and physicist Erwin Schrödinger proved it with a famous thought experiment.

Imagine sealing a cat in a box with a 50% chance that a random event kills it within the hour. Until you open the box, the cat's fate is undetermined — physics only lets you say it is in some sense both alive and dead at once, a blend of both possibilities, until the moment you look and the outcome resolves into one.

Electrons behave the same way. An electron doesn't sit at one fixed point on a tidy orbit. Until it's measured, its position is a blur of probabilities — not "somewhere we haven't checked yet," but genuinely undetermined. Schrödinger and physicist Werner Heisenberg proved mathematically that we can only ever predict the region an electron is likely to occupy, never its exact position and motion at the same instant.

☁

This is why modern chemistry replaced Bohr's neat rings with the electron cloud: a fuzzy region where an electron is probably found. Where the cloud is densest, the odds of finding the electron there are highest.

Concept Two

An Electron's Home Address

Scientists still need a way to describe roughly where an electron lives, even if they can't pin down an exact spot. So they give every electron an address, built the same way your home address is: broad category first, more specific each step.

The building analogy

Think of an atom like an apartment building. Electrons fill the lowest, most stable floors first, just like tenants take the ground floor before the penthouse.

Principal quantum number (n)

The floor number: a whole number from 1 to 7. Higher n = farther from the nucleus = more energy.

Subshell

The room type on that floor: s, p, d, or f. Each type has its own shape and its own room count.

Orbital

A single room — a region where an electron is most likely to be found. Holds at most 2 electrons.

Electron spin

The two electrons sharing an orbital must have opposite spins, shown as ↑ and ↓ — never two arrows pointing the same way.

Concept Two, continued

The Four Room Shapes

Every subshell has a distinct 3D shape and a fixed number of orbitals (rooms), which sets its maximum electron capacity.

p  ·  dumbbell

d  ·  cloverleaf

f  ·  complex

Concept Three

The Filling Rules

Like any building, this one has strict move-in rules. Three principles govern every atom in the universe.

Aufbau Principle

Electrons fill the lowest-energy orbitals first, floor by floor, before any higher floor opens up.

"Aufbau" is German for "building up."

Pauli Exclusion Principle

An orbital holds at most 2 electrons, and those two must have opposite spins (↑↓).

No two electrons in an atom are ever perfectly identical.

Hund's Rule

Within a subshell, electrons spread across separate orbitals first before any pair up.

Like passengers taking empty rows before sharing a seat.

The one tricky exception: the diagonal rule

Energy levels overlap in a way that surprises most students: the 4s orbital is actually lower energy than 3d, so it fills first — even though "4" is a bigger number than "3." This single exception is the reason the periodic table has the shape it does, as you'll see shortly.

Fill order for the first 8 subshells

Follow the arrow: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p. Notice 4s fills before 3d — that's the exception in action, and the same zig-zag pattern keeps going for every element on the table.

Concept Four

Writing Electron Configurations

An electron configuration is the complete address list for every electron in an atom, written in fill order. Each atom's configuration is unique to it — and to its element.

Worked example: Oxygen (8 electrons)

  1. Find the atomic number. Oxygen's is 8, so a neutral oxygen atom has 8 electrons to place.8 electrons total
  2. Follow the fill order from the diagram above, filling each subshell to its max before moving on: 1s holds 2, 2s holds 2 — that's 4 placed, 4 left for 2p.1s² 2s² → 4 electrons placed, 4 remaining
  3. Place the rest in 2p (which can hold up to 6).1s² 2s² 2p⁴

Check your work: add up the superscripts. 2 + 2 + 4 = 8. That matches oxygen's atomic number, so the configuration is correct.

💡

Connecting back to what you already know: when oxygen gas glows in a neon-style tube, one of its 2p electrons is briefly kicked up to a higher, unoccupied address — an excited state. It doesn't stay there. It falls back to 1s²2s²2p⁴, its ground state, and releases a photon whose energy exactly equals the gap between the two addresses. That's the same quantized-light idea from your last unit, now explained by electron addresses.

Try it yourself: Sodium (11 electrons)

Fill in order: 1s² (2) → 2s² (4) → 2p⁶ (10) → 3s¹ (11).

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹ — check: 2+2+6+1 = 11 ✓

Notice sodium's very last electron sits alone in 3s — that lone outermost electron will turn out to matter a lot once we reach the periodic table.

Concept Five — The Big Connection

From Addresses to the Periodic Table

Here's the payoff. The periodic table isn't an arbitrary grid — its exact shape is a map of electron addresses. Every row, column, and block traces directly back to everything above.

The periodic table, colored by which subshell is filling
s-block — filling an s subshell p-block — filling a p subshell d-block — filling a d subshell f-block — filling an f subshell

Each row (period) is a new floor. The d-block appears starting on row 4, not row 3 — because of that 4s-before-3d exception you just learned.

A period (row)

Each new row means a new principal quantum number, n, has started — a new floor of the building.

A block

Which region of the table an element sits in tells you which subshell its highest-energy electrons are filling.

A group (column)

Elements in the same column have the same number of outermost, or valence, electrons — which is why they behave alike.

Valence electrons

The electrons in the highest occupied energy level. They're the ones involved in bonding, and they drive the periodic trends up next.

Concept Five, continued

A Sneak Peek: Periodic Trends

You'll study these in depth soon, but you already have everything you need to predict them, because each one is a direct consequence of electron addresses.

Two directions, two trends

Atomic radius

Shrinks across a period: more protons pull the same energy level's electrons in tighter. Grows down a group: each row adds a whole new, farther-out floor.

Ionization energy

The energy to remove an electron. It rises across a period, because electrons are held closer and tighter to the nucleus.

Electronegativity

How strongly an atom pulls electrons in a bond. It also rises across a period, for the same reason: a tighter grip on the outer floor.

Notice the pattern: every trend traces back to one idea — how tightly the nucleus holds its outermost electrons. That's electron addresses in action.

Why It Matters

Real World: Electron Addresses at Work

Fireworks and neon signs

Copper compounds glow blue-green, strontium glows red, sodium glows yellow-orange. Same idea every time: heat kicks an electron to a higher address, it falls back, and releases a photon whose color is fixed by that element's exact energy gaps.

Quantum dot displays

Quantum dot TVs use nanocrystals so small that squeezing an electron into them changes its energy gap. Smaller dot, bigger gap, bluer light. Larger dot, smaller gap, redder light — engineers tuning electron addresses on purpose.

💻

Silicon chips: silicon sits in the p-block with exactly 4 valence electrons — the same reason every silicon atom in your phone's processor bonds the same predictable way. Chip designers rely on that valence-electron pattern, straight from silicon's spot on the periodic table, to build billions of identical, reliable connections.
