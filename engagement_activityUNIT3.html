What's Really In Your Sweat?

Using what you already know about atoms and electrons to start asking bigger questions about how matter is put together

You're in the 85th minute. The match is tied. Your legs feel like concrete, you're cramping, and your brain is starting to fog. Your coach sends in a sub — but not before handing you a bottle of Gatorade.

Here's the question: what is actually in that bottle, and why does it help?

The answer has everything to do with atoms — which you already know something about. Today we're going to use what you know to start building toward a much bigger idea: how atoms connect to each other to form the substances that make up everything around you, including your own body.

Your Starting Point

What You Already Know

✅ Background You're Bringing Into This

Atoms are made of protons, neutrons, and electrons. The number of protons determines what element it is.

Electrons are arranged in energy levels (shells) around the nucleus. The outermost electrons are the most important for how an atom behaves.

The periodic table is organized by those outer electrons — elements in the same column behave similarly because they have the same number of outer electrons.

Electron configuration tells you exactly how electrons are distributed across an atom's energy levels.

The Question This Unit Will Answer

If atoms are the building blocks, how do they stick together to form the substances that actually exist in the world — water, salt, sugar, the air you breathe, the proteins in your muscles?

The Science

What's Actually in Sweat & Sports Drinks

Scientists have analyzed sweat from elite soccer players during high-intensity matches. Players can lose 2–3 liters of sweat per match — and what leaves with that fluid isn't just water. It's a mix of different substances, each one made of specific atoms arranged in a specific way.

Look at the table below. You'll notice that each substance has a chemical formula — a shorthand that tells you exactly which atoms are in it, and how many of each.

Formula Substance What it does in your body during a match
NaCl Sodium chloride (table salt) Maintains fluid balance; the main substance lost in sweat
KCl Potassium chloride Keeps muscles contracting and heart rhythm steady
MgSO₄ Magnesium sulfate Prevents cramping; supports many chemical reactions in cells
C₆H₁₂O₆ Glucose (sugar) Direct fuel for muscles and the brain
H₂O Water Carries everything; regulates body temperature
NaHCO₃ Sodium bicarbonate Neutralizes acid buildup in muscles; delays that burning feeling in your legs
Your Tasks

Breakout Room — Work Through These Together

📋 Pick ONE substance from the table. Work through the questions below for that substance.
1

Read the formula. What atoms are in your substance, and how many of each?

Use the chemical formula — each letter represents a different element, and the number after a letter tells you how many atoms of that element are present. If there's no number, it means 1.

Example: H₂O means 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom.

2

Look up your elements on the periodic table. What do you notice about them?

Find each element in your formula on the periodic table. Note which group (column) each one is in. Are they metals, nonmetals, or a mix? Are they close together on the table or far apart? What does their position tell you about their outer electrons?

3

Here's the big mystery: these atoms don't just sit next to each other — they're held together somehow. What do you think is doing that?

You know that atoms have electrons in their outer shells. You know that the periodic table is organized by those outer electrons. Based on that — what's your best guess for what's holding these atoms together in your substance? You don't need a right answer. Just reason it out with your group.

4

What would actually happen to a player if your substance completely disappeared from every sports drink on Earth right before a big match?

Be as specific as you can. Use what you know from your own experience as a player — you've likely felt some of these effects before. This is the "so what" of chemistry: it always connects back to something real.

★

Bonus — if your group has time:

Look at all six substances in the table. Some have only two or three different atoms. Some, like glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), have a lot. Why do you think some substances are simple and others are complex? What might that mean for how hard or easy they are for the body to use?
