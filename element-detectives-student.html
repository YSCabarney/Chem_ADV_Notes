Element Detectives: The Lithium Supply Chain Crisis

A real shortage is reshaping the global battery industry. Your team has the evidence — now use the periodic table to crack the case.

⏱ 15–20 min total 👥 Zoom breakout rooms 3 roles per team

Your mission briefing

What's going on?

Right now, in 2026, the global race to build electric vehicles and store renewable energy has created a massive demand for lithium — the key element inside rechargeable batteries. Lithium supplies are tight, prices have spiked, and several countries have accused battery suppliers of sending shipments that aren't what they claimed to be.

Your team has received three mystery element samples from a suspected shipment fraud. The supplier claims all three samples are lithium. Your job: use the data provided and the periodic table to figure out which samples are real — and what the fake ones actually are.

What you already know (no chemistry vocabulary needed)

Every element has a unique set of properties — its atomic number, mass, reactivity, and location on the periodic table. Like a fingerprint, these can't be faked. A substance claiming to be lithium either is lithium, or it's something else entirely.

Step 1 — Before breakout rooms (2 min)

Assign your roles

Each person on your team takes one role. You'll each focus your analysis through that lens, then combine findings.

🔬 Property Scout

You compare the physical properties of each sample to what we know about lithium. Does it look and behave right?

🔢 Number Cruncher

You focus on the numeric data — atomic mass, atomic number — and check the math against the periodic table.

🗺 Table Navigator

You use the periodic table to locate candidates. What other elements are near lithium? Could a fake blend in?

⏱ ~10 min PHASE 2 — Breakout Rooms: Analyze the Evidence

Your teacher will move you into breakout rooms now. Work through the three evidence files below together. Each person should focus on their role, but everyone records answers.

Your reference tool — the periodic table

Use this simplified version as your guide. Key elements to locate: H (1), Li (3), Be (4), Na (11), K (19), B (5), Mg (12).

Evidence files — three mystery samples

Read each file carefully. Your supplier claims all three are lithium. Lithium's known properties: atomic number 3, atomic mass ≈ 6.94 u, soft silvery metal, extremely reactive with water, lowest density of all solid metals (0.534 g/cm³), burns with a crimson-red flame.

EVIDENCE FILE A — Sample Alpha

Lab report shows atomic number: 3. Atomic mass recorded: 6.94 u. Sample is soft and silvery. When a small piece was dropped in water, it reacted vigorously and floated. Flame test produced a crimson-red color. Mass: 4.2 g. Volume by displacement: 7.9 cm³. Calculated density: 0.53 g/cm³.

EVIDENCE FILE B — Sample Beta

Lab report shows atomic number: 3. Atomic mass recorded: 6.94 u. Sample appears silvery but is harder than expected. Flame test: yellow-orange. Mass: 5.1 g. Volume by displacement: 2.4 cm³. Calculated density: 2.13 g/cm³. Reacted slowly with water — much less vigorously than Sample Alpha.

EVIDENCE FILE C — Sample Gamma

Lab report shows atomic number: 3. Atomic mass recorded: 6.94 u. Sample is soft and silvery. Flame test produced a crimson-red color. However, a mass spectrometer reading showed an unusual isotope ratio — far more ⁷Li (mass 7) than expected. Supplier could not explain the ratio. Density: 0.535 g/cm³.

Your team's analysis questions

Work through these together in your breakout room. Write your team's answers in the fields below.

Q1 — Property Scout: Which sample(s) have physical properties that DON'T match lithium? What's the giveaway?
Q2 — Number Cruncher: Sample Beta's density is 2.13 g/cm³. Lithium's is 0.534 g/cm³. If the atomic number is really 3, what could explain such a different density?
Q3 — Table Navigator: Use the partial periodic table above. Which element near lithium has a yellow-orange flame color and a density around 0.97 g/cm³? (Hint: same group as lithium, one row below.)
Q4 — Everyone: Sample Gamma has the right density and flame color, but an unusual isotope ratio. Is it real lithium? Explain your reasoning.
Q5 — Team verdict: Which samples are genuine lithium? Which is fake, and what do you think it actually is?

Stuck? Use this hint

Look at Group 1 (the leftmost column). Lithium (Li, #3) and sodium (Na, #11) are neighbors in the same group. They're both reactive metals. The biggest differences between them are their density, atomic mass, and flame color. Sodium burns yellow. Lithium burns crimson-red. One of these samples may be sodium masquerading as lithium.

What you just did

You used element position, physical properties, density, flame tests, and isotope ratios to identify an unknown material. Real forensic chemists do exactly this — using mass spectrometry and elemental analysis to authenticate materials, catch fraud, and analyze evidence. The periodic table is their map.
