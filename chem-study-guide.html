Before You Start

The Big Idea: Models Change

A model is a scientist's best explanation of something too small (or too far away) to see directly. As tools improve and new evidence comes in, models get updated — not scrapped.

Think about phones: a flip phone, a first-generation smartphone, and today's phone all do the same basic job — communicate — but each new version built on the last one's ideas and fixed what didn't fit anymore. The atom has gone through the same process. Every scientist in this guide was partially right, and each new discovery added a piece the last model was missing.

i

Keep this in mind as you read: you'll meet five different "pictures" of the atom. None of them is wrong exactly — each one is what the best available evidence supported at the time.

Concept One

Dalton's Atomic Theory

In 1803, John Dalton couldn't see a single atom — microscopes weren't nearly strong enough. Instead, he measured things he could observe (temperature, gas behavior, how substances combined) and reasoned backward to five rules that still hold up today.

1 · Atoms exist

All matter is built from extremely small particles called atoms. The word comes from Greek for "cannot be cut."

2 · Same element, same atom

Every atom of a given element is identical in size and mass. Every silver atom is exactly like every other silver atom.

3 · Atoms aren't destroyed

In chemical reactions, atoms can't be created, destroyed, or split. (We'll bend this rule later — carefully.)

4 · Whole-number ratios

Atoms of different elements combine in simple whole-number ratios. There's no such thing as half an atom in a compound.

5 · Reactions rearrange

Chemical reactions combine, separate, or rearrange atoms — they don't create new ones.

🌊 Real world

Water is always 2 hydrogen atoms + 1 oxygen atom (H2O) — never 2.5 or 1.7. That fixed ratio is rule 4 in action.

★

Real-world check: rule 3 says atoms can't be split — and for chemical reactions (burning, rusting, digesting) that's still true. It took a whole new kind of reaction — nuclear reactions, like the ones powering the sun — to actually split an atom. Dalton wasn't wrong; he just hadn't seen nuclear chemistry yet.

Concept Two

Building a Better Atom

Dalton pictured the atom as a solid, featureless ball — like a marble. Over the next 130 years, four more discoveries cracked that marble open.

1897Thomson finds the electron

Firing cathode rays (glowing beams, like old TV tubes used), J. J. Thomson found tiny negatively-charged particles inside the atom — electrons. His model: negative specks scattered through a positive "pudding."

1911Rutherford finds the nucleus

Rutherford fired charged particles at gold foil, expecting them to pass straight through. Some bounced straight back — proof of a small, dense, positively-charged center. He named it the nucleus.

1913Bohr adds energy levels

Working with Rutherford's data and the colors given off by excited gases, Bohr proposed electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed energy levels — not scattered randomly, and not free to have just any amount of energy.

1932Chadwick finds the neutron

Chadwick, who once worked in Rutherford's lab, bombarded beryllium with particles and isolated a neutral particle with about the same mass as a proton — the neutron, completing the nucleus.

1920sSchrödinger & Heisenberg: the electron cloud

Electrons don't orbit like tidy planets — their exact position can't be pinned down. Instead, we can only predict a probability of where an electron might be: the electron cloud. Where the cloud is densest, an electron is most likely to be found.

This is the model chemists actually use today — Bohr's rings are still drawn because they're a useful simplification, similar to how a subway map isn't geographically exact but is still useful.

Concept Three

Meet the Subatomic Particles

Every atom is built from just three particles. Where a particle lives and what charge it carries determines everything about how atoms behave.

The one rule that never breaks: the number of protons identifies the element, like a name tag that can't be removed. Change the proton count and you have a completely different element. Electrons, on the other hand, come and go easily — which is exactly what makes the next section possible.

Concept Three, continued

Ions & Isotopes

Protons are locked in place, but electrons and neutrons can vary — and each kind of variation has its own name.

Neutral atom vs. ion

More protons than electrons after the loss = a net positive charge. Gaining an electron instead would flip the sign to negative.

⚡

Ion — electrons change

Gain or lose electrons and the atom's charge changes. Lose an electron → more protons than electrons → positive ion. Gain one → negative ion.

Rub a balloon on your hair — electrons transfer, both objects become ions, and you get static cling.
⚖️

Isotope — neutrons change

Same element (same protons), different number of neutrons — so a different mass. The charge doesn't change, only the mass.

Carbon-14 is a heavier isotope of carbon used to date fossils; americium-241 is the isotope inside most smoke detectors.
🧂

Both, working together

Table salt forms because sodium loses an electron (becomes a positive ion) and chlorine gains one (becomes a negative ion) — opposite charges attract and bond.

Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl

Concept Four

Light Behaves Like a Wave

Electrons don't just sit still — when they move, they interact with light. Before we get to that, you need the vocabulary to describe any wave, light included.

Anatomy of a wave

Wavelength: distance between two matching points on the wave. Frequency: how many full waves pass a point each second (Hz). Amplitude: the wave's height, measured from the centerline.

📻

Real world: tuning a radio means picking a frequency — "101.5 FM" is 101.5 million waves passing per second. Ocean waves work the same way: tall, closely-spaced waves (high amplitude, short wavelength) carry more energy and hit the shore harder than small, spread-out ones.

Wavelength and frequency are opposites

Squeeze a wave's wavelength shorter, and more waves have to pass by each second — frequency goes up. Stretch the wavelength longer, and frequency drops. They move in opposite directions, which becomes important in the next section.

Concept Four, continued

The Electromagnetic Spectrum

Visible light — the light your eyes can detect — is only a thin sliver of all the possible wavelengths. The full range is the electromagnetic spectrum (EMR), and unlike sound or ocean waves, every part of it can travel through the vacuum of space with no medium needed.

Longer, lower-energy waves → shorter, higher-energy waves

Same rule as the wave section, applied to light itself: shorter wavelength means higher frequency — and, as you'll see in the formula section, higher energy too.

Concept Five

Formulas That Connect It All

Two short equations tie every idea above together — from a wave's speed to the exact energy of a single particle of light.

Speed of Light
c = λf
c = speed of light, a constant 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s  ·  λ (lambda) = wavelength (m)  ·  f = frequency (Hz)

Because c never changes, wavelength and frequency must always trade off — exactly the inverse relationship from the wave section, now written as math.

Worked example

A microwave oven emits waves with a frequency of 1.00 × 10¹⁰ Hz. What's the wavelength?

  1. Start with c = λf, and rearrange to solve for λ — just like manipulating any equation, divide both sides by f.λ = c / f
  2. Substitute the known values.λ = (3.00 × 10⁸ m/s) / (1.00 × 10¹⁰ Hz)
  3. Calculate.λ = 0.0300 m = 3.00 cm
Energy of a Photon
E = hf
E = energy (Joules)  ·  h = Planck's constant, 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s  ·  f = frequency (Hz)

Energy is directly proportional to frequency — the opposite relationship from wavelength. Chain both formulas together and you get the rule that runs the whole unit: shorter wavelength → higher frequency → higher energy.

Try it: energy of a red-light photon

Red light has a frequency of about 4.60 × 10¹⁴ Hz. E = hf = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)(4.60 × 10¹⁴ Hz)

E ≈ 3.05 × 10⁻¹⁹ J — a tiny amount of energy, but multiply it by trillions of photons and you get everything from a sunburn to a laser cutting steel.

Concept Six — Bringing It Together

Photons, Excited Atoms & the Aurora

Here's where electrons, energy levels, and light all meet. When an electron absorbs energy, it jumps to a higher energy level — its excited state. That's unstable, so it quickly falls back to its original, lowest-energy ground state — and when it falls, it releases the extra energy as a single particle of light: a photon.

Absorb energy, jump up · release a photon, fall back

The photon's energy exactly equals the gap between the two levels — which is why every element releases its own specific, predictable colors.

Atomic fingerprints

Because each element's electrons sit at their own unique energy levels, each element releases its own unique set of colors — its emission spectrum. Run an element's light through a spectroscope and the pattern of colored lines identifies it as reliably as a fingerprint.

Hydrogen's emission spectrum

Every hydrogen atom in the universe — in a lab, in the sun, in a distant star — produces exactly this pattern. Different elements produce entirely different patterns of lines.

🎆

Real world: fireworks get their colors from metal-salt electrons falling back to ground state — copper glows blue-green, strontium glows red, sodium glows yellow. A neon sign works the same way: electrons in excited neon gas fall back and release that signature orange-red glow.

Case study: why does the aurora glow?

Recent solar storms have pushed the aurora borealis (northern lights) far further south than usual, lighting up skies that don't normally see it — and it's this exact process, playing out 60–300 km above your head:

  1. Charged particles blast out from the sun in a solar wind and get funneled toward Earth's poles by the planet's magnetic field.
  2. Those particles slam into oxygen and nitrogen atoms in the upper atmosphere, transferring energy to their electrons — knocking them into an excited state.
  3. The electrons fall back to ground state and release that energy as photons. Oxygen near 100 km up glows green; oxygen higher up glows red; nitrogen glows blue and purple.

Every vocabulary word in this guide — atom, electron, energy level, photon, wavelength, emission — is doing real work in that explanation. That's not a coincidence: it's the whole unit, compressed into one glowing sky.
