Section 1 · How scientific knowledge grows

The nature of science

Scientific knowledge is not a list of permanent facts. It is the best explanation so far, and it gets stronger every time new, independent evidence supports it. Our model of the atom has been revised at least five times, and each revision came from a new experiment.

DurableKnowledge that has held up to testing over a long time. It is trusted, but it can still be revised if strong new evidence appears.
RobustKnowledge supported by many independent lines of evidence and different methods, all pointing to the same conclusion.
Empirical evidenceData collected through observation and experiment, not opinion or authority.
ReplicationOther scientists repeat an experiment and get the same results.
Peer reviewExperts check a study's methods and conclusions before it is published.
Scientific modelA representation used to explain something too small, large, or complex to observe directly.
What strengthens vs. weakens a scientific claim
Makes knowledge strongerDoes NOT make knowledge stronger
Independent scientists repeat the experiment and get the same resultA famous or powerful person agrees with it
New, different kinds of evidence point to the same conclusionMany people share it online
The study passes peer reviewIt has never been tested but "sounds right"
The claim makes predictions that turn out to be correctData that disagree are ignored or hidden
Watch out"Durable" does not mean "can never change." Durable and robust knowledge stays open to revision when strong new evidence appears.

Section 2 · Evidence → model

How the atomic model changed

On the test, you'll be asked which evidence supports which scientist's idea. For each scientist, learn three things: the experiment, what was observed, and what was concluded.

1803 · John Dalton
Solid sphere model. Elements combine in fixed whole-number ratios, so matter must be made of tiny, indivisible atoms.
1897 · J.J. Thomson
Plum pudding model. Cathode rays bent toward a positive plate, and this happened no matter which metal he used. So every atom contains small negative particles: electrons.
1911 · Ernest Rutherford
Nuclear model. Most alpha particles passed straight through gold foil, but a few bounced back sharply. So an atom is mostly empty space with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.
1913 · Niels Bohr
Planetary (energy level) model. Heated hydrogen gives off only certain colors of light (a line spectrum). So electrons can only have specific energies: fixed energy levels.
1932 · James Chadwick
Neutron discovered. Nuclei had more mass than their protons could explain, and a neutral radiation was found that could knock protons loose. So the nucleus also holds neutral particles: neutrons.
1920s · Schrödinger & Heisenberg
Quantum mechanical (electron cloud) model. An electron's exact path can't be known, only the probability of where it is.

Section 5 · Electrons & light

Energy levels, photons & emission

Ground stateThe lowest energy level an electron (or atom) can have. Its most stable state.
Excited stateAny energy level higher than the ground state, reached after absorbing energy.
QuantumThe smallest exact amount of energy an electron can gain or lose. Energy comes in packets, not a smooth flow.
PhotonA particle (packet) of light that carries one quantum of energy.
Emission spectrumThe unique set of colors an element gives off. It works like an element's fingerprint.
AbsorptionAn electron takes in energy and jumps up to a higher level.

Animation · Jump up, fall down

Move electron to:
The electron starts in the ground state (n = 1). Pick a higher level to excite it.
Moving UP (low → high)The electron absorbs energy. The atom is now in an excited state.
Moving DOWN (high → low)The electron releases energy as a photon of light. The bigger the drop, the higher the photon's energy.

Section 6 · The modern atom

Electron clouds, orbitals & valence electrons

Bohr drew electrons on neat circular paths. The quantum mechanical model replaced those paths with electron clouds: regions where an electron is likely to be found. The denser the cloud, the more likely the electron is there.

Electron cloudThe fuzzy region around a nucleus showing where electrons are most likely to be found.
OrbitalA 3-D region in an atom where there is a high probability of finding an electron. Each orbital holds at most 2 electrons.
Principal quantum number (n)The number (1, 2, 3, …) for an electron's main energy level. Higher n means farther from the nucleus and higher energy.
SublevelA shape type of orbital within an energy level: s, p, d, or f.
Valence electronsThe electrons in the outermost energy level. They control bonding and reactivity.
Octet ruleAtoms are most stable with 8 valence electrons (a full outer shell). For the first shell, full means 2.

Short-response practice

Write your answer on paper first, then open the model answer to check.

Explain how the quantum mechanical model describes electrons differently than Bohr's model.
Bohr placed electrons in fixed circular orbits at set distances. The quantum mechanical model (Schrödinger) says we can't know an electron's exact path. Instead it describes an electron cloud: a region of probability where the electron is likely to be. Orbitals are the regions within that cloud, and each holds up to 2 electrons.
Describe what an orbital is and how many electrons it can hold.
An orbital is a three-dimensional region around the nucleus where there is a high probability of finding an electron. Each orbital holds a maximum of two electrons with opposite spins. Orbitals come in shapes such as s (sphere) and p (dumbbell).
What does the principal quantum number tell you about an electron?
The principal quantum number (n) is a whole number (1, 2, 3, …) that tells which main energy level an electron is in. As n increases, the electron is farther from the nucleus, has more energy, and the energy level can hold more electrons.

Short-response practice

Why are halogens never found as single, uncombined atoms in nature?
Halogens (group 17) have 7 valence electrons, one short of a stable octet. Gaining one electron fills their outer shell, so they are highly reactive. They quickly bond with other atoms, or pair up as molecules like Cl₂. Because of this they are found in compounds such as NaCl, not as lone atoms.
Use valence electrons to compare the reactivity of group 2 and group 18 elements.
Group 2 (alkaline earth metals) have 2 valence electrons, which they lose fairly easily to reach a stable configuration. That makes them reactive and found only in compounds. Group 18 (noble gases) already have a full outer shell (8, or 2 for helium). They have no need to gain or lose electrons, so they are unreactive and exist alone in nature.

Section 8 · Patterns that repeat

Periodic trends

Atomic radiusThe size of an atom: the distance from the nucleus to the outer electrons.
Ionization energyThe energy needed to remove an electron from an atom.
ElectronegativityHow strongly an atom pulls on electrons in a chemical bond.
ShieldingInner electrons block the outer electrons from the nucleus's pull.
Nuclear chargeThe positive pull of the protons. More protons means a stronger pull.
Metallic characterHow easily an atom loses electrons and behaves like a metal.
Trend summary: memorize this table
PropertyAcross a period (left → right)Down a group (top → bottom)Why
Atomic radiusDecreases ↓Increases ↑Across: more protons pull the same shell in tighter. Down: more shells are added.
Ionization energyIncreases ↑Decreases ↓Across: electrons are held tighter. Down: outer electrons are farther away and more shielded.
ElectronegativityIncreases ↑Decreases ↓Same reasons as ionization energy (fluorine is the highest).
Metallic characterDecreases ↓Increases ↑Down: electrons are lost more easily.

Animation · How ion formation changes size

Cations are smallerA metal atom loses electrons, and often its whole outer shell. The remaining electrons are pulled in tighter, so the positive ion is smaller than its atom.
Anions are largerA nonmetal gains electrons. The extra electrons repel each other and the same protons must hold more electrons, so the negative ion is larger than its atom.

Section 9 · Science, ethics & sources

Science and society

Discoveries about the atom can be used to help people or to harm them. Several scientists tried to steer how their work was used.

Scientists and the use of their discoveries
Scientist(s)DiscoveryHow they tried to influence its use
Marie & Pierre CurieRadioactivity; the elements polonium and radiumChose not to patent their method for isolating radium, so researchers and doctors everywhere could use it freely (e.g., to treat cancer).
Otto Hahn, Fritz Strassmann & Lise MeitnerNuclear fission: a large nucleus splitting into smaller ones (1938)Meitner declined to take part in military projects. Hahn later signed public statements with other scientists urging that the discovery be used only for peaceful purposes.
Albert EinsteinE = mc² (a small amount of mass equals a huge amount of energy)Wrote to the U.S. president warning of the discovery's possible military use. He later regretted his role and spent his final years speaking out for peace and international cooperation.
