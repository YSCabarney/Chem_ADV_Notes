1What Is Matter?

Everything you can touch, taste, breathe, or weigh is made of matter.

Definition

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space (volume).

Your chair, the air in the room, the water in a bottle, and the aluminum in a soda can are all matter. Light, heat, sound, and gravity are not matter — they have no mass and occupy no volume.

Chemists sort all matter into a branching system. At every branch you ask one yes/no question:

  1. Is the composition fixed everywhere in the sample? → separates pure substances from mixtures.
  2. Can it be broken into simpler substances by a chemical reaction? → separates compounds from elements.
  3. Is the sample uniform all the way through? → separates homogeneous from heterogeneous mixtures.
From CK-12 Chemistry

The three common states of matter — solid, liquid, and gas — describe physical form, not chemical identity. Water is the same substance (H2O) whether it is ice, liquid, or steam. Classification of matter is about what a sample is made of, which does not change when it melts or boils.

2The Classification Flowchart

Matter splits into pure substances and mixtures. Pure substances split into elements and compounds. Mixtures split into homogeneous and heterogeneous.

Study tip

Read the chart as a decision tree: "Is it uniform and fixed everywhere?" If yes → pure substance, then ask "Can a reaction break it down?" If a sample's makeup can vary from spoonful to spoonful, it is a mixture.

3Pure Substances

Definition

A pure substance is matter that has a fixed, uniform composition and a definite set of properties. It is made of a single element or a single compound.

  • Gold (Au) is a pure substance made of a single element — every atom in a gold bar is a gold atom.
  • Glue is a pure substance too: it is made of many molecules of the same compound, polyvinyl acetate.
  • Distilled water, table sugar (sucrose), and baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) are pure substances that are compounds.

The key idea: a sample of a pure substance is identical everywhere. A gram taken from the top of a sugar jar has the same composition and properties as a gram from the bottom.

From CK-12 Chemistry

Every pure substance has characteristic properties — a specific melting point, boiling point, and density — that never change and can be used to identify it. Pure water always freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at sea level. If a "water" sample boils at 102 °C, something is dissolved in it, so it is a mixture, not a pure substance.

4Elements

Definition

An element is a pure substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by any chemical reaction. Each element is made of only one kind of atom.

All 118 elements on the periodic table are pure substances. Examples: iron (Fe), oxygen (O2), helium (He), carbon (C), copper (Cu), gold (Au).

Common mix-up

Oxygen gas is written O2 because its atoms bond in pairs, but it is still an element — both atoms are oxygen. A substance is a compound only when it contains different elements bonded together.

5Compounds & the Law of Definite Proportions

Definition

A compound is a pure substance made of two or more elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio. The smallest unit of a molecular compound is a molecule.

Examples of compounds: water (H2O), carbon dioxide (CO2), table salt (NaCl), baking soda (NaHCO3), glucose (C6H12O6), and ethanol/alcohol (C2H6O).

A compound's properties are brand new

When elements bond, the compound behaves nothing like its parts:

Table 5.1 — Elements vs. the compound they form
ElementOn its ownElementOn its ownCompound formed
Sodium (Na)Soft metal that explodes in waterChlorine (Cl)Poisonous yellow-green gasNaCl — edible table salt
Hydrogen (H)Flammable gasOxygen (O)Supports burningH2O — water, puts out fire
From CK-12 Chemistry — Law of Definite Proportions

Also called the law of constant composition: a given compound always contains its elements in the same proportion by mass. Every sample of pure water is 11.2% hydrogen and 88.8% oxygen by mass, whether it came from a river in Brazil or a comet. This fixed ratio is exactly what makes a compound a pure substance and not a mixture — you cannot make "extra-oxygen water."

Real-world example

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) vs. water (H2O). Same two elements, different fixed ratio → completely different compounds. Water is safe to drink; hydrogen peroxide disinfects cuts and bleaches hair. One extra oxygen atom per molecule changes everything.

6Mixtures

Definition

A mixture forms when two or more substances (solids, liquids, or gases) are physically combined without chemically bonding. Each substance keeps its own composition and properties, and the ratio can vary.

  • Components can be the same state (two gases in air) or different states (sugar solid + water liquid).
  • No chemical reaction occurs, so mixtures can be pulled apart by physical methods.
  • The proportions are not fixed — coffee can be weak or strong; both are still "coffee."
Real-world example

Ocean water is a mixture of pure water plus dissolved salts, gases, and minerals. Evaporate it and the water leaves as vapor while solid salt stays behind — proof the salt was never chemically bonded to the water. Salinity differs from the Baltic Sea to the Dead Sea, which is why the proportions of a mixture are described as "variable."

7Homogeneous vs. Heterogeneous Mixtures

Definitions

A homogeneous mixture is uniform — the components are blended so evenly that any sample looks and behaves the same, even under a microscope. A heterogeneous mixture is not uniform — you can see (or scoop) different regions with different composition.

Homogeneous mixtures

Just one uniform phase is visible. Dissolving sugar in water makes a homogeneous mixture — the sugar "disappears" and every sip tastes equally sweet. Other examples: air, perfume, bleach, pulp-free orange juice, steel, brass, vinegar, gasoline. A homogeneous mixture of substances that stay mixed indefinitely is called a solution.

Heterogeneous mixtures

Different components are unevenly spread. In a bag of trail mix, heavier peanuts sink and lighter raisins ride on top. Vegetable soup is heterogeneous because each spoonful catches a different mix of vegetables and broth. Other examples: salad dressing (oil + vinegar), granite, rocks, cereal in milk, sand, concrete, pizza, muddy water.

Quick test

Could you point to two spots in the sample that look or taste different? Yes → heterogeneous. Looks identical no matter where you sample it (even under magnification)? Homogeneous.

8Solutions, Colloids, Suspensions & Emulsions

Homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures can be sorted further by particle size and by whether the parts stay mixed or settle out.

Solutions — solute + solvent

Key vocabulary

In a solution, the solute is the substance being dissolved (present in the smaller amount) and the solvent is the substance doing the dissolving (present in the larger amount). In saltwater, salt is the solute and water is the solvent.

Solution particles are individual atoms, ions, or molecules — far too small to see or filter out. Solutions can be:

  • Gaseous: air (oxygen and other gases dissolved in nitrogen).
  • Liquid: saltwater, soda (CO2 gas dissolved in flavored water), vinegar.
  • Solid: alloys such as brass (copper + zinc melted together, then cooled) or stainless steel (iron + chromium + nickel).
From CK-12 Chemistry — air composition

Dry air is a homogeneous solution of gases: about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.9% argon, and 0.04% carbon dioxide, plus trace gases. Because it is a mixture, these percentages can shift slightly (more CO2 in a crowded room), unlike the fixed ratio in a compound.

Colloids

A colloid contains particles that are bigger than solution particles but still too small to settle out. Colloids look uniform, but because some regions can hold more suspended particles than others, chemists classify them as heterogeneous. Examples: milk, fog, whipped cream, gelatin, smoke, paint, butter.

From CK-12 Chemistry — the Tyndall effect

Shine a flashlight through a true solution (saltwater) and the beam is invisible — the particles are too small to scatter light. Shine it through a colloid (milk, fog) and the beam lights up as a visible path. This light-scattering is the Tyndall effect, and it is a quick lab test to tell a colloid from a solution. It is why car headlights are visible in fog and why the sky looks hazy.

Suspensions

From CK-12 Chemistry

A suspension has particles large enough to see and heavy enough to settle out if left alone (and they can be removed by filtering). Muddy water, Italian salad dressing, and some medicines ("shake well before use") are suspensions. This is the biggest-particle category: solution < colloid < suspension.

Emulsions

An emulsion is a mixture of two liquids that normally do not mix (like oil and water). A third substance, an emulsifier, holds them together. In mayonnaise, egg yolk is the emulsifier that lets oil and water-based vinegar stay blended. Milk is a natural emulsion of butterfat in water.

9Mixture vs. Compound — The Big Comparison

This is the distinction students miss most often. A compound is a pure substance; a mixture is not.

Table 9.1 — Mixture and compound comparison
 MixtureCompound
CompositionVaried — there is no set ratio in how the substances mix.Definite — the atoms are bonded in specific ratios given by the chemical formula.
PropertiesEach substance keeps its own chemical and physical properties.The compound has entirely different properties from the elements it contains.
BondingThe substances are not chemically bonded together.The elements are chemically bonded together.
SeparationCan be separated by physical means (filtering, evaporating, etc.).Can only be separated into elements by a chemical change (reaction).
Energy to separateLittle or none.Usually a large amount (electricity, heat, another reactant).
ExamplesAir, most rocks and ores, saltwater, brass, trail mix.Water (H2O), carbon dioxide (CO2), sodium chloride (NaCl).
Real-world example

Sand + iron filings is a mixture — drag a magnet through it and the iron jumps out, unchanged, in seconds (a physical separation). Rust (iron oxide, Fe2O3) is a compound — the iron and oxygen are bonded, and getting the iron back requires a blast furnace running a chemical reaction at ~1,500 °C.

10Separating Mixtures

Physical separation

Because the parts of a mixture are not bonded, they can be sorted using differences in physical properties — size, mass, density, magnetism, boiling point, or solubility. Examples from everyday life:

  • Picking your favorite fruit out of a fruit salad and leaving the rest.
  • Boiling saltwater so the water evaporates and salt crystals stay behind.
  • Opening a soda: lowering the pressure lets dissolved CO2 gas escape from the liquid.

Heterogeneous mixtures are usually easy (sort by size, color, or magnetism). Homogeneous mixtures take more work — heat, pressure changes, or density differences.

Chemical separation

A compound can only be broken into its elements by a chemical reaction. Table salt (NaCl) is made of sodium and chlorine; splitting it requires electrolysis (running electricity through molten salt). Passing electricity through water breaks it into hydrogen and oxygen gas.

Table 10.1 — Common mixture-separation techniques

FiltrationParticle sizeA liquid (filtrate) passes through filter paper; solids (residue) are trapped.Coffee makers, water-treatment sand filters, kidney dialysis.
CentrifugationDensityRapid spinning forces denser components to the bottom of the tube.Blood separated into plasma and cells; cream spun from milk.

Real-world example — one mixture, three steps

Turning seawater into fresh water and salt: (1) filter out sand and seaweed, (2) distill the clear brine — water vapor rises and condenses as fresh drinking water, (3) the evaporation residue left in the flask is sea salt. No chemistry required, because seawater is a mixture.

11Physical vs. Chemical Properties & Changes

From CK-12 Chemistry — why this matters here

The whole classification system rests on the difference between physical and chemical change. Physical means separate mixtures; chemical means separate compounds. Knowing which is which tells you which branch of the flowchart you are on.

Table 11.1 — Physical vs. chemical
 PhysicalChemical
PropertyObserved without changing the substance's identity: color, density, melting point, hardness, state, magnetism, solubility.Describes how a substance reacts to form new substances: flammability, reactivity with acid, ability to rust or tarnish.
ChangeSame substance, new form: melting ice, dissolving sugar, crushing a can, boiling water.New substance(s) form: burning wood, rusting iron, baking a cake, digesting food, electrolysis of water.
SignsReversible by physical means; no new substance.Often shows: gas bubbles, light or heat, color change, precipitate, odor; hard to reverse.
Role in classificationSeparates the parts of a mixture.Separates a compound into elements, or builds a compound from elements.
Watch out

Dissolving salt in water is a physical change — evaporate the water and the salt returns unchanged. Electrolyzing that water into H2 and O2 gas is a chemical change — brand-new substances, not reversible by drying.

13Vocabulary Glossary

Matter
Anything that has mass and takes up space.
Pure substance
Matter with a fixed, uniform composition; an element or a compound.
Element
A pure substance made of one kind of atom that cannot be chemically broken down.
Compound
A pure substance of two or more elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio.
Molecule
The smallest unit of a molecular compound that still has its properties.
Mixture
Two or more substances physically combined, not bonded; each keeps its own properties; ratio varies.
Homogeneous mixture
A mixture that is uniform throughout (also called a solution when it stays mixed).
Heterogeneous mixture
A mixture with visibly different regions or composition.
Solution
A homogeneous mixture of a solute dissolved in a solvent.
Solute
The substance being dissolved (smaller amount).
Solvent
The substance doing the dissolving (larger amount).
Alloy
A solid solution of two or more metals (e.g., brass, steel, bronze).
Colloid
A mixture with mid-sized particles that do not settle; shows the Tyndall effect; classified as heterogeneous.
Suspension
A mixture with large particles that settle out and can be filtered.
Emulsion
A mixture of two normally unmixable liquids, held together by an emulsifier.
Tyndall effect
The scattering of a light beam by colloid particles, making the beam visible.
Physical separation
Splitting a mixture using physical property differences; no new substances.
Chemical separation
Splitting a compound into elements via a chemical reaction.
Filtration
Separating an insoluble solid from a liquid using filter paper.
Distillation
Separating liquids by differences in boiling point via evaporation and condensation.
Chromatography
Separating components by their different attraction to a stationary surface vs. a moving solvent.
Centrifugation
Using rapid spinning to separate components by density.
Electrolysis
Using electricity to drive a chemical reaction, e.g., splitting water or molten salt.
Law of definite proportions
A given compound always contains the same elements in the same mass ratio.

14Self-Check Questions

Try these — answers below
  1. Classify each: (a) copper wire, (b) sugar water, (c) chicken noodle soup, (d) carbon dioxide, (e) 18-karat gold.
  2. A clear liquid boils completely at exactly 78 °C, leaving nothing behind. Is it more likely a pure substance or a mixture? Explain.
  3. You have a beaker of sand, salt, and iron filings in water. List the steps and techniques to separate all four components.
  4. Why is oxygen gas (O2) an element but carbon dioxide (CO2) a compound, even though both contain oxygen atoms bonded together?
  5. Milk and saltwater both look uniform. How could you tell them apart with a flashlight, and what is that test called?
  6. Explain, using the law of definite proportions, why H2O and H2O2 are different substances.
Answer key
  1. (a) element • (b) homogeneous mixture (solution) • (c) heterogeneous mixture • (d) compound • (e) homogeneous mixture (alloy).
  2. Pure substance — a single sharp boiling point with no residue means fixed composition (this is ethanol). A mixture would boil over a range and/or leave residue.
  3. Use a magnet to remove iron filings. Filter the rest to catch sand (residue); saltwater passes through (filtrate). Evaporate or distill the filtrate: collect pure water from the vapor, and salt is left behind.
  4. O2 contains only oxygen atoms — one element. CO2 contains two different elements (carbon and oxygen) bonded together — that makes it a compound.
  5. Shine the beam through each. It stays invisible in saltwater (true solution) but glows as a visible path in milk (colloid). The test is the Tyndall effect.
  6. Each compound has one fixed mass ratio of its elements. Water is always 2 H : 1 O per molecule; hydrogen peroxide is always 2 H : 2 O. Different fixed ratios = different compounds with different properties.
